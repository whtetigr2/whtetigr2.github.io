A level, settled

Walk around. Every wall you see was decided by a lattice relaxing, not by a ray being traced — and what each wall is made of comes out of a second lattice, twelve numbers per material. No image, sprite or texture is stored anywhere on this page. It is shaped like the shooters it is imitating. It contains none of their data.

What is input, and what is inferred

Walking a ray through the level grid to ask is there material at this point is a lookup. That is occupancy, and it is the input — the same way a prompt is input to a sampler. It gets recomputed as you move, because moving changes which points the rays pass through.

Deciding which of those materials you can actually see is a different question, and that is the one being sampled. Each screen column is a chain of bits meaning this ray got this far still travelling, and the surface you see is wherever the chain turned off.

A reported wall is a strong bias on its cell, not a hard constraint. That matters: a constraint cannot be overruled, so one bad cell in the occupancy would become a wall in front of you. A bias can be outvoted by the cells around it, which is what the couplings are there to do. Measured on this level, agreement with the true nearest surface over 200 columns, seven runs of 150 sweeps each:

clean — couplings off100%
clean — full lattice100%
8% noise — couplings off3.2%
8% noise — full lattice99.2%

On a clean input the couplings are worth nothing — 100% either way, in all seven runs. When the occupancy already contains the answer, a linear scan recovers it and the lattice is an expensive way to match that. Sampling does not win the easy case.

What it buys is robustness to an input that lies. At 8% misreported cells the scan drops to 3.2% and the lattice holds 99.2%, because walls being continuous and rays not restarting are facts that no single cell holds and no scan can use. Turn the noise up and switch the couplings off to watch the difference directly — that gap is the lattice's entire contribution.

Those two coupling strengths were tuned against ground truth rather than by eye. The first version of this page used a coherence term about half its best value, which cost roughly thirty points of accuracy on a noisy input and nothing at all on a clean one — so nothing on screen looked wrong, and only measuring against the true surface showed it.

Both numbers are reproducible on this page: open a console and run ablate(). It re-settles the lattice from random for every cell of the table and prints it.

The chains are not restarted between frames. They carry over and keep relaxing while you move, so the picture is a sampler tracking a changing constraint rather than a fresh solve every frame. Turn quickly and you can watch the far walls resolve a beat behind you. That lag is the sampler working, not a dropped frame.

The creatures

A creature's body is sampled rather than drawn. Fifteen by twenty-one spins relax under a field built from ten numbers — where a horn is wanted, where a head is, how far the shoulders run, where the arms hang clear of the torso, where the legs split. That field is written by hand from the idea of a hunched horned biped. It is a preference, not a stencil: it says where material is favoured and the sampler decides what actually condenses.

Individuality comes from somewhere else — one seed per creature, which fixes a quenched random field it is then sampled under. Same ten species numbers, different seed, different body. Measured across individuals, 11% of the 315 cells differ from one to the next, against about 7% of cells moving between one moment and the next — so each is a recognisable individual, and the flicker at its edges is the sampler's own thermal noise at β = 1, not an animation. shape(0) in a console prints one as text; try a few indices.

Eleven numbers total, then — ten for the species and one for the individual — in place of a stored image. There is no sprite here to differ from, and none was read to build the field.

Bilateral symmetry is carried by that field rather than by a coupling between a cell and its mirror. Both halves of the anatomy field and of the quenched field are the same numbers reflected, so the body comes out bilateral before any coupling is applied. A mirror coupling would also be illegal here: at an odd width a cell and its mirror always share the (x+y)&1 parity this lattice sweeps on — 294 of 315 pairs — so a two-colour sweep would update them together while they were coupled, which block Gibbs does not permit and no two-colour hardware blocking allows.

What they do is sampled too. Each carries two spins, advance and break sideways, drawn from a field that sees four things: how close you are, whether it has line of sight to you, what it decided a moment ago, and what the creatures near it decided. Line of sight is an occupancy lookup walked along a segment — a scan, and the right tool for the job. It is input here, not inference.

The last term is the only one that is not a property of a single creature, and the only reason the group is more than eight independent things. Pack coupling above turns it off. Measured in a fixed arena, eleven runs a cell, four repeats:

decisions swing together — off2.84×
decisions swing together — on3.64×
arrival spread — off2.13 s
arrival spread — on1.95 s

These are small effects and they are worth stating as small. The first row is the honest one: the uncoupled figure is 2.84×, not 1.0×, because eight creatures reacting to the same player already move somewhat together without any coupling between them at all. The coupling adds to that; it does not create it. It also shaves about a fifth of a second off when the group reaches you and tightens the spread by roughly eight per cent — consistent in direction across all four repeats, and nothing more dramatic than that. packTest() in a console re-runs the table.

Shooting, and why the noise slider reaches it

A shot is resolved against the depth the visibility lattice settled on, not against a fresh raycast. What you can hit is what the lattice says you can see. That makes the sensor-noise slider a gameplay control rather than a diagnostic: standing still in the hall, aimed at a wall 10.6 units away,

8% noise, couplings off — lattice says2.1
8% noise, couplings on — lattice says10.8
shots stopped in empty air — off12 / 12
shots stopped in empty air — on0 / 12

With the couplings off and a noisy input, the lattice invents a wall about two units in front of you and every shot stops in mid-air against it. Turn them on and the real wall comes back — and stays back at 12% noise too. Same ablation as the table further up, arriving as something you can feel rather than a number you have to trust.

The same models, sampled by somebody else's library

The point of writing a world as an energy instead of as code is that the energy is portable: anyone's sampler can run it. So both models here were exported from this page while it was running, compiled through tsu-compiler, and handed to thrml — Extropic's own sampling library — which had never seen them.

visibility lattice11,200 spins · 22,144 couplings
thrml vs this page100.0%
creature lattice315 spins · 594 couplings
per-cell marginals, thrml vs this page0.007

Identical occupancy in, identical depth profile out — every column. The creature models are stochastic, so those are compared as per-cell probabilities over many draws instead: the two samplers agree to 0.007 on average. Both lattices came out degree 4 and two-colourable, which is the shape block Gibbs needs.

That port is also what found a defect here. The creature model originally coupled each cell to its mirror, and the compiler refused to two-colour it — correctly, because the sweep was updating coupled cells together and the centre column was coupled to itself. The model was wrong and the compiler was right. It is fixed above, and the symmetry it was supposed to provide was already in the field.

Everything else in here is deliberately not an energy. The level geometry, the doors, the line-of-sight test, the shot resolution — those are lookups, and a lookup written as an energy is just a lookup with extra steps.

Simulated throughout. thrml ran on a CPU; no thermodynamic hardware was involved and no claim is made about one.

Why it cannot be anybody's level

There is no pixel data here. Surfaces are coupling constants — twelve numbers for a material. Nothing on screen is loaded from anywhere.

The layout is hand-drawn in the spirit of the genre: rooms, a corridor, a courtyard, a zigzag. It is not anyone's map, because reproducing one would need their data, and the point of the exercise is not to have any.

Method and controls for the offline version are in tsu-compiler, under audit/visibility_as_inference.py and audit/texture_from_energy.py.

What this is, and what it is not

Everything here runs in simulation — in your browser, or on a CPU. Nothing on this site has been run on thermodynamic hardware. No TSU silicon exists for me to test on, so no timing, energy, or throughput figure here describes a physical device, and none is offered as one.

Every number is my own measurement unless it is attributed to someone else, in which case it is theirs and is cited as theirs. I do not treat any of it as established fact until somebody outside this project reproduces it. Where a claim of mine has failed that test, it is corrected in public rather than removed — the repositories keep the retractions alongside the results.

If you find something here that is wrong, that is a favour and I would like to hear it.
