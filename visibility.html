A renderer computes visibility: march along a ray, stop at the first wall. This does not. The geometry is written down as an energy, and which surface you see is whatever the lattice settles into — and so is what it is made of. Turn up the sensor noise and the couplings start doing real work; leave it at zero and they barely matter, which the page says plainly below. A correction, and it is the interesting part

This page used to say that occlusion here was a constraint the energy will not let you break. That was wrong, and it was wrong in a way worth showing rather than quietly editing out.

Walls were originally clamped off — held at "stopped" and unchangeable. With a clamp, every cell becomes independent: free space says “travelling” with probability 0.999994, a wall says “stopped” absolutely, and reading off the first stop is simply reading off the first wall. The couplings between cells contributed nothing. Switching them off changed the answer by zero. It was a linear scan for the nearest wall with a lattice running uselessly beside it — a raycast in a different notation.

Every check passed, too: agreement hit 100%, no ray stopped in free space, the cold control sat at 0%. All of those are consistent with couplings that do nothing, because none of them asked whether the couplings mattered. The missing test was an ablation — turn a term off and see if anything changes.

Where a lattice actually earns its keep

Make a wall a strong bias instead of an absolute clamp and the couplings have something to do: they can outvote a wall the input got wrong. Turn up Sensor noise above and the input starts reporting walls in empty space — the failure a scan cannot survive, because a scan stops at the first thing it is told about. Then press Couplings: on / off and watch the difference appear.

Measured on this page — 96 columns, seven runs of 150 sweeps each, agreement with the true nearest surface:

clean input, couplings off100.0%
clean input, full lattice99.9%
8% noise, couplings off11.9%
8% noise, full lattice83.8%

Read the first two rows before the second two. On a perfect input the couplings do not help — they cost a tenth of a point. When the input already contains the answer, a scan is the right tool and a lattice is slightly worse than decoration. There is no reading of that table on which sampling wins the easy case, and pretending otherwise is how the last version of this page went wrong.

What the lattice buys is what a scan cannot have at any price: tolerance for an input that lies. At 8% misreported cells it takes 11.9% to 83.8%, because walls are continuous, rays do not restart, and neighbouring columns see similar things — facts that live in no single cell and that a first-hit scan has no way to use. That is the whole claim, and it is narrower than the one it replaces.

You do not have to take the table on faith. Open a console on this page and run ablate(); it re-settles the lattice from a random start for every cell and prints the numbers above.

The full write-up and the offline version with its controls live in tsu-compiler, under audit/visibility_as_inference.py.

What this is, and what it is not

Everything here runs in simulation — in your browser, or on a CPU. Nothing on this site has been run on thermodynamic hardware. No TSU silicon exists for me to test on, so no timing, energy, or throughput figure here describes a physical device, and none is offered as one.

Every number is my own measurement unless it is attributed to someone else, in which case it is theirs and is cited as theirs. I do not treat any of it as established fact until somebody outside this project reproduces it. Where a claim of mine has failed that test, it is corrected in public rather than removed — the repositories keep the retractions alongside the results.

If you find something here that is wrong, that is a favour and I would like to hear it.
